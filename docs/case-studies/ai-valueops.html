01 · Problem framing

The problem is not a shortage of AI ideas. It is a shortage of validated outcomes.

Leaders may fund pilots without a clear business owner, baseline, workflow integration plan, adoption target, or agreed definition of value. A polished demo can look successful while saving no meaningful time, improving no customer outcome, and creating new risk or support costs.

Business user pain

Teams do not know which AI tools are approved, when to use them, whether outputs can be trusted, or how the tool fits into daily work.

Leadership pain

Executives cannot compare initiatives consistently, see blockers early, or distinguish projected savings from verified benefits that reach the P&L.

02 · Discover before building

Start with one expensive, frequent workflow

I would not build an enterprise-wide AI dashboard first. I would select one workflow where delays, rework, or service costs are measurable and where AI can be evaluated against a non-AI baseline.

2Baseline cost
3Score use case

Discovery questions

  • Which workflow is costly, frequent, slow, or error-prone?
  • Who experiences the pain, who owns the outcome, and who can approve a change?
  • What is the current baseline for cycle time, cost per case, quality, rework, and user satisfaction?
  • What data, security, privacy, compliance, and human-review constraints apply?
  • What would make the team stop the pilot rather than scale it?
03 · Product solution

Build an AI initiative control tower

Portfolio prioritisation

  • Capture the problem statement, affected users, workflow owner, sponsor, and expected business outcome.
  • Score impact, feasibility, data readiness, adoption effort, risk, and time-to-value.
  • Compare initiatives using a consistent rubric and make assumptions visible.
  • Require a baseline and success criteria before approving a pilot.

Execution and value tracking

  • Track pilot stage, accountable owner, dependencies, blockers, spend, and decision date.
  • Monitor eligible users, active usage, workflow completion, override rate, quality, and support burden.
  • Separate projected benefits, observed pilot effects, and finance-validated realised value.
  • Record the decision: scale, iterate, pause, or stop, with evidence and rationale.
04 · Prioritisation model

Make trade-offs explicit

05 · Pilot and experiment

Prove the workflow improves, not just that the model responds

Example pilot plan

  1. Choose one bounded workflow and a named business owner.
  2. Document current volume, cycle time, quality, cost, and user experience.
  3. Run a time-boxed pilot with a defined eligible user group and human review where appropriate.
  4. Compare against baseline or a control group, accounting for seasonality and case mix.
  5. Review quality, errors, adoption, time saved, and downstream process outcomes.

Metrics and guardrails

  • Adoption: eligible users activated, weekly active users, repeat workflow usage.
  • Efficiency: cycle time, handling time, rework, cost per completed case.
  • Quality: acceptance rate, correction rate, error severity, customer satisfaction.
  • Economics: model/API costs, implementation and support costs, verified net benefit.
  • Guardrails: privacy incidents, policy violations, harmful errors, user complaints, and human-override rate.

Targets should be set from the workflow baseline before the pilot. I would not promise a generic ROI percentage across different use cases.

06 · ROI that finance can trust

Separate activity, impact, and realised value

Time saved is not automatically cash saved. To estimate value responsibly, quantify the hours actually released, the share converted into productive capacity or avoided cost, incremental operating costs, quality changes, and any downstream revenue or customer impact. Finance should validate the calculation and assumptions.

Illustrative value equation

Net realised value = validated benefits − implementation costs − ongoing AI costs − added review, support, and risk costs.

Report projected, pilot-observed, and finance-validated realised value separately. Include the measurement period, eligible population, comparison method, and confidence level.

07 · Operating model

Every pilot needs a decision owner

Roles

  • Business owner: accountable for the workflow outcome and adoption.
  • Product manager: problem framing, prioritisation, user experience, roadmap, metrics, and decision log.
  • Engineering / AI: integration, evaluation, reliability, monitoring, and cost.
  • Risk / security / legal: appropriate controls, review, and approval.
  • Finance: validates value assumptions and realised benefits.

Decision cadence

  • Weekly pilot review: adoption, quality, blockers, costs, and incidents.
  • At the pilot end: compare results with pre-agreed success criteria.
  • Scale only when value, user adoption, reliability, and risk are acceptable.
  • Iterate if evidence identifies a solvable blocker; stop if value or feasibility is weak.
What this demonstrates

How I would approach the challenge as a PM

Translate an executive-level AI concern into one measurable workflow problem; align an accountable owner; build a transparent prioritisation framework; partner across business, engineering, finance, and risk; measure adoption and quality; validate financial impact; and make an evidence-based scale, iterate, or stop decision.

Prajakta’s product takeaway

AI value is proven in business outcomes, not pilot counts. A strong AI portfolio product connects each use case to an accountable owner, a measurable baseline, adoption evidence and a scale, iterate or stop decision. The PM’s job is to turn experimentation into disciplined capital allocation.
