Turn approved credit-card applicants into active, repeat users

A product case study on one commercially important problem: customers get approved, but too many fail to activate their card and make a first purchase. The goal is to identify the friction, design a responsible activation journey, and measure whether it creates durable value.

01 · Problem framing

Approval is not the same as customer value

A credit-card product can acquire and approve a customer, yet still fail to create value if the customer does not understand the next step, cannot access the card, feels uncertain about fees or repayment, or simply has no clear reason to use it. Pushing spend without understanding the cause can create complaints, poor-quality usage, and risk.

Customer problem

“I was approved, but I’m not sure what happens next, how to access my card, or whether using it will cost me anything unexpected.”

Business problem

Approved accounts that never activate create acquisition and underwriting cost without generating sustainable engagement or revenue.

Events and cuts I would inspect

  • Approval-to-card-access conversion and time elapsed between events.
  • Activation success/failure by device, channel, verification outcome, and card type.
  • First-purchase conversion by time since activation and merchant/category.
  • Support contacts, failed attempts, declined transactions, and stated reasons for non-use.
  • Complaints, fraud signals, repayment understanding, and early delinquency guardrails.
03 · Product hypothesis

Make the next safe step obvious

If a meaningful share of approved users drop before their first purchase because setup is unclear or trust is low, a contextual activation checklist may outperform generic reminders.

Proposed experience

  1. Show a clear approval-success screen with the exact next action.
  2. Guide eligible users through card access and required verification, with actionable error recovery.
  3. Explain fees, due dates, repayment expectations, and how to avoid interest in plain language.
  4. Offer a user-controlled first-use checklist and a reminder only if the user has not completed the step.
  5. After the first transaction, show a clear confirmation and where to track spending and payment due dates.

What I would not do

  • Use misleading urgency or imply a purchase is required to keep approval.
  • Incentivise spending before customers understand repayment obligations.
  • Send repeated notifications without consent, relevance, or frequency caps.
  • Optimise activation while ignoring fraud, complaints, credit losses, or customer outcomes.
04 · Experiment plan

Test the smallest useful intervention

ElementPlan
PopulationNewly approved users who are eligible for the card-access journey and have not activated.
ControlExisting approved-user experience.
TreatmentClear next-step checklist, contextual help, transparent repayment information, and one consent-aware reminder.
Primary metricShare of eligible approved users completing activation and first purchase within 7 days.
Secondary metricsTime to activation, card-access completion, support-contact rate, and 30-day repeat use.
GuardrailsFraud, disputes, complaints, opt-outs, failed payments, early delinquency, and risk-adjusted contribution.
MethodRandomised experiment with pre-defined eligibility, sample-size calculation, duration, and decision thresholds. Check results by key customer segments.

I would set the minimum detectable effect and sample size using baseline conversion and traffic before launch, rather than inventing a target uplift after seeing results.

05 · Prioritisation

Ship in stages, with clear decision gates

First 2 weeks: learn

  • Validate event quality and build the funnel baseline.
  • Review support reasons and interview a small set of eligible non-activators.
  • Align Product, Engineering, Design, Risk, Compliance, Operations, and issuer partners on constraints.

Next 2–4 weeks: test

  • Prototype and usability-test the checklist and recovery states.
  • Instrument events, launch a controlled experiment, and monitor guardrails.
  • Roll out only if activation improves without unacceptable customer or risk harm.
06 · Success definition

Activation is the start, not the finish

I would recommend scaling only when the experiment shows a credible improvement in first-use conversion and the cohort demonstrates healthy subsequent engagement. The decision should account for incremental activation, repeat use, service cost, rewards or incentives, fraud, credit losses, and risk-adjusted contribution margin.

Decision rule

Scale if the primary metric improves against the pre-registered threshold, the result is statistically credible for the planned test, and all risk/customer guardrails remain within agreed limits. Iterate if the result is inconclusive or the funnel reveals a more specific blocker. Roll back if customer harm or risk guardrails breach.

What this demonstrates

How I would operate as the PM

Start with a measurable customer and business problem; validate it with behavioural and qualitative evidence; translate the diagnosis into a narrow product intervention; align cross-functional owners; run a controlled experiment; and make a scale, iterate, or stop decision using both growth and risk outcomes.

Prajakta’s product takeaway

Approval is a milestone; the first successful, safe use is the outcome. Activation improves when users understand the next step, complete setup with less friction and reach a meaningful first transaction. A controlled experiment should improve activation without weakening fraud, credit or customer-support guardrails.
